# Compliance Manager Interview

> **Simulation Note:** This is a fictional stakeholder interview created for a portfolio project. The stakeholder responses below were generated by ChatGPT based on the project scenario. I developed the discovery approach and questions and used the simulated responses as source material for my analysis. Findings and project decisions based on these interviews are documented separately in the [Discovery Findings](../03-discovery-findings.md).

## Interview

### 1. Does the current problem statement align with your understanding?

**Compliance Manager:**

Generally, yes, although I would be careful about describing the difference between our numbers and Customer Service's numbers as an inconsistency that necessarily needs to be corrected.

Compliance tracks a specific subset of complaints because we have regulatory and risk responsibilities that Customer Service doesn't have. I would expect our numbers to be different.

Where I think we do have a problem is that there isn't a clear relationship between the different sets of information. Leadership might see 140 complaints in one report and 18 in another without understanding whether those 18 are included in the 140, completely separate, or some combination of both.

I agree that we need a more consistent enterprise view, but I would not want that to come at the expense of the controls we have around regulatory complaints.

### 2. How does Compliance define a complaint?

**Compliance Manager:**

Our definition is broader in some ways and narrower in others.

For regulatory purposes, we generally care about expressions of dissatisfaction related to a bank product, service, practice, or employee when the issue may indicate regulatory, legal, fair-lending, consumer-protection, or reputational risk.

The customer does not necessarily need to explicitly ask us to resolve something for us to care about it.

For example, if a customer says they believe they were treated differently during a lending decision because of a protected characteristic, that needs Compliance attention even if they aren't asking the representative to do anything.

We also sometimes review issues that wouldn't necessarily be classified as complaints by Customer Service if they indicate a potential compliance concern.

And not every Customer Service complaint belongs in our process. Someone being unhappy because a branch closed five minutes earlier than they expected might be a legitimate service complaint, but it probably isn't something Compliance needs to track.

### 3. How do complaints get into the Compliance process?

**Compliance Manager:**

Several ways.

Customer Service and branches can escalate issues to us. Other departments can refer something directly. We also identify complaints through other compliance monitoring activities occasionally.

When we receive something, someone on the Compliance team reviews it and determines whether it needs to become a Compliance complaint case.

If it does, we create a record in our compliance tracking system. We document the complaint type, applicable regulatory category, customer information when available, the date received, source, assigned owner, relevant dates, investigation notes, disposition, and whether further escalation was required.

There may also be supporting documentation attached to the case.

### 4. What happens when Customer Service escalates a complaint to Compliance?

**Compliance Manager:**

This is an area where I think we could improve the process.

Customer Service sends us the issue, and then Compliance evaluates it. Sometimes we need additional information from Customer Service or the customer. We may also need information from another department.

If it meets our criteria, we create a Compliance case and manage it through our process.

The Customer Service complaint and Compliance case are currently separate records in separate systems. There isn't a shared identifier connecting them.

Sometimes someone manually includes the Customer Service complaint number in the Compliance notes, but I wouldn't say that happens consistently.

That's one reason reconciliation is difficult. If you handed me the Customer Service spreadsheet and our spreadsheet, there isn't always a reliable field I could use to say, "These two records represent the same underlying customer issue."

### 5. What regulatory or business rules affect how Compliance tracks complaints?

**Compliance Manager:**

We need to be able to demonstrate that certain complaints were identified, investigated, and handled appropriately.

Dates are important. We need to know when an issue was received, when it was assigned, when required actions occurred, and when it was closed.

Classification is also important because different types of complaints may have different review or escalation requirements.

We need sufficient documentation to understand what happened and support our disposition.

Access is another consideration. Some complaint records can contain sensitive customer information, allegations involving employees, legal issues, or information related to investigations. Not everyone who needs aggregate complaint reporting should necessarily be able to see those details.

Retention requirements also apply to some of our records.

I would want Compliance involved before any changes are made to the fields, classifications, or workflows associated with regulatory complaints.

### 6. How do you currently create Compliance complaint reporting?

**Compliance Manager:**

We export information from the compliance tracking system and prepare our reporting separately.

Most of our reporting is focused on regulatory and risk oversight rather than general customer experience. We look at complaint types, status, aging, escalation, significant issues, and sometimes trends.

There is manual work involved, although our biggest concern isn't really the time spent creating the report. It's making sure the information is accurate and appropriately interpreted.

We sometimes get questions because our totals don't match Customer Service. Then we have to explain that we're reporting a different population.

Occasionally we do need to compare records manually if leadership is asking about a particular issue or discrepancy.

### 7. We've heard that Customer Service sometimes doesn't record an issue if it is resolved immediately. Does that matter from a Compliance perspective?

**Compliance Manager:**

Potentially, yes.

Whether the customer's issue was resolved quickly doesn't determine whether there was a compliance concern.

If a customer says, for example, that they believe they were discriminated against during a lending interaction, resolving some immediate service issue wouldn't eliminate our need to know about the allegation.

That's why training and escalation criteria are important. Frontline employees need to recognize certain types of issues even if they can resolve the customer's immediate problem.

I wouldn't necessarily say every one of those interactions needs a Customer Service complaint record. That's something we'd need to work through. But if it meets Compliance escalation criteria, we need a reliable way for it to reach us.

### 8. How do you think about negative feedback from sources like app-store reviews?

**Compliance Manager:**

I wouldn't automatically exclude it because it's anonymous.

From a compliance perspective, the source matters less than the content of the allegation.

If someone posts a review saying, "The app is terrible and crashes constantly," that's probably not a Compliance issue.

If someone posts, "This bank is deliberately preventing disabled customers from accessing their accounts through the app," I would want someone to flag that for review even if we don't know who posted it.

Whether we'd create a formal Compliance complaint case would depend on what information we had and what our investigation found.

So I would distinguish between something that should be monitored or reviewed and something that becomes a formal Compliance case.

### 9. Customer Service raised a distinction between "what constitutes a complaint" and "what constitutes a complaint that can be managed as an individual Customer Service case." Does that distinction make sense from your perspective?

**Compliance Manager:**

Yes, and I think that's an important distinction.

I would actually go a little further and say we may be trying to make one word—"complaint"—represent several related concepts.

There is the customer's expression of dissatisfaction.

There is a Customer Service complaint case.

There is a Compliance complaint case.

There may also be broader customer feedback that indicates an emerging issue but doesn't become either type of case.

Those things can overlap without being identical.

My concern with creating one bank-wide definition would be if we accidentally force all of those concepts into one workflow. I think we need consistent terminology and relationships between the concepts, but that doesn't necessarily mean every department needs the exact same process or case definition.

That may have contributed to the previous standardization effort struggling.

### 10. What would success look like from your perspective?

**Compliance Manager:**

First, leadership understands what the numbers mean. If they see a total for Customer Service complaints and a total for Compliance cases, they should understand the relationship between them.

Second, we have a reliable way to identify when the same underlying issue exists in multiple systems. I don't want Compliance analysts manually comparing customer names, dates, and descriptions to determine whether two records are related.

Third, regulatory complaint handling remains controlled and auditable.

I'd also like better visibility into emerging patterns. If Customer Service or Digital Banking is seeing something repeatedly that could eventually represent a compliance risk, it would be useful for us to see that earlier.

And access needs to be appropriate. Executives may need to know that a category of regulatory complaints is increasing without having access to sensitive case-level details.

### 11. Are there any other considerations you think I should know about?

**Compliance Manager:**

I would involve Data Governance heavily in the terminology discussion.

I would also avoid starting with the assumption that the solution is simply to combine all of these records into one dataset. We need to understand the relationships first.

One issue can potentially generate multiple records. One customer can have multiple complaints. Multiple customers can complain about the same underlying product problem. And one complaint can involve multiple products, channels, or concerns.

If we don't model those relationships correctly, we could create a dashboard that looks authoritative while actually producing misleading counts.

I'd also make sure we establish ownership. If leadership disagrees with a complaint category or definition six months from now, someone needs to own that business rule and the process for changing it.

Those are probably my biggest concerns: preserving regulatory controls, defining the relationships between different complaint concepts, appropriate access, and clear ownership of the resulting business rules.
