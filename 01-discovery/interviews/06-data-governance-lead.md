# Data Governance Lead Interview

> **Simulation Note:** This is a fictional stakeholder interview created for a portfolio project. The stakeholder responses below were generated by ChatGPT based on the project scenario. I developed the discovery approach and questions and used the simulated responses as source material for my analysis. Findings and project decisions based on these interviews are documented separately in the [Discovery Findings](../03-discovery-findings.md).

## Interview

### 1. Does the current problem statement align with your understanding?

**Data Governance Lead:**

Yes, although I think we've learned from the previous attempt that the problem isn't simply that different departments have inconsistent definitions.

Different teams have legitimate reasons for looking at customer complaints differently. The governance problem is that those differences haven't been formally defined or connected.

Right now, someone can use the word "complaint" in a meeting and Customer Service, Compliance, and Digital Banking may each be talking about something slightly different without realizing it.

I think the goal should be to establish enough shared terminology and structure that those differences are understood and the data can be used together appropriately.

### 2. Can you tell me what happened during the previous attempt to standardize complaint reporting?

**Data Governance Lead:**

The effort started with a similar concern: leadership was receiving different complaint numbers from different areas and wanted a single enterprise number.

The initial approach was essentially to create one standard definition of a complaint and have everyone align their reporting to it.

That's where we ran into trouble.

Customer Service wanted the definition to reflect complaints they could capture and manage operationally. Compliance needed to preserve its regulatory definitions and processes. Other teams had customer feedback that didn't fit neatly into either model.

The conversations became focused on whose definition was "correct."

We also moved into defining fields and categories before we had really agreed on the underlying business concepts.

Eventually the project lost momentum because changing everyone's operational processes would have required significant effort, and there wasn't enough agreement that doing so would actually improve the information.

### 3. Based on what we've learned so far, it appears that "complaint" may be describing several related concepts. How would you approach that from a governance perspective?

**Data Governance Lead:**

I think that's a much better starting point than trying to create one definition.

We should identify the distinct business concepts first.

For example, we might ultimately distinguish between something like:

- A customer feedback signal
- An expression of dissatisfaction or complaint
- A Customer Service complaint case
- A Compliance case
- An underlying customer issue or incident

I'm not saying those should be the final terms. We'd need the stakeholders to agree on them.

But once we've identified the concepts, we can define what each one means and how they relate.

Then we can answer questions like:

- Can one complaint create multiple cases?
- Can one case contain multiple complaints?
- Can multiple customer complaints relate to the same underlying issue?
- When does feedback become a complaint?
- When does a complaint require a Customer Service case?
- When does something require Compliance review?
- Which concepts need unique identifiers?

That's much more useful than arguing about one universal complaint definition.

### 4. Who should own these definitions and business rules?

**Data Governance Lead:**

That's something we'd need to establish explicitly.

Data Governance can facilitate the process, document the definitions, and make sure they're managed consistently, but I wouldn't want Data Governance independently deciding what constitutes a Customer Service complaint or a regulatory case.

The business needs to own the meaning of the data.

For an enterprise concept like "customer complaint," we may need shared ownership or a designated business owner with representatives from Customer Experience, Customer Service, Compliance, Digital Banking, and potentially Retail Banking involved in governance decisions.

Department-specific concepts should have clear owners within those departments.

We'd also need a change process. Definitions and categories will evolve. If someone changes a category in Customer Service six months from now, we need to understand whether that affects enterprise reporting and other teams.

### 5. How should we think about authoritative data sources when information about the same customer issue exists in multiple systems?

**Data Governance Lead:**

I would avoid trying to designate one system as authoritative for the entire concept of a customer complaint.

Authority may need to exist at the attribute or business-concept level.

For example, the Customer Service system might be authoritative for the status of a Customer Service complaint case.

The Compliance system should remain authoritative for the status and disposition of a Compliance case.

Digital Banking may be authoritative for the original app feedback and associated technical context.

A separate enterprise data layer could potentially establish relationships between those records without replacing the operational systems that own them.

We need to be precise about what we mean by "source of truth." There may not be one source of truth for the entire customer complaint lifecycle.

### 6. What data-quality concerns stand out to you based on what we've learned so far?

**Data Governance Lead:**

Several.

The inconsistent categorization in Customer Service is an obvious one, especially if representatives interpret categories differently.

Missing complaint records are another issue. If complaints are sometimes resolved without being recorded, reporting based solely on complaint cases won't represent every complaint interaction.

Duplicates and repeat contacts need to be understood. We need to distinguish between a duplicate record and a legitimate second interaction about the same issue.

Incomplete branch referrals and inconsistent notes affect our ability to understand and classify records.

There are also cross-system issues. Without a shared identifier, we can't reliably connect a Customer Service complaint to a Compliance case or another downstream record.

Digital feedback creates additional challenges because some records are anonymous and different sources contain different attributes.

I'd want us to profile the actual data before deciding how significant each issue is. Stakeholder perceptions are useful, but we should validate them against the data.

### 7. How should categories be handled if different departments need different classifications?

**Data Governance Lead:**

They don't necessarily need to use the same operational categories.

Trying to force one category list across Customer Service, Compliance, and Digital Banking could make the categories less useful for everyone.

Instead, we could potentially create an enterprise classification that maps departmental categories into broader shared concepts.

For example, Digital Banking might need detailed categories like authentication, mobile deposit, and application performance. Customer Service might organize those differently.

An enterprise reporting layer could map both into something broader such as Digital Banking while preserving the more detailed source classifications.

But I wouldn't design that mapping until we've looked at the actual values and understood how the teams use them.

And someone would need to own and maintain those mappings.

### 8. What should we consider when connecting records across systems?

**Data Governance Lead:**

First, we should distinguish between deterministic relationships and inferred relationships.

If Customer Service escalates a complaint to Compliance, ideally the systems would preserve an explicit identifier showing that relationship. That's much stronger than trying to infer the relationship later using customer ID, dates, and text.

For historical records, we may have to infer some relationships, but we'd need to be transparent about the confidence of those matches.

We also shouldn't assume records should be deduplicated simply because they appear related.

A Customer Service complaint and a Compliance case might represent the same customer issue, but they're still two legitimate business records serving different purposes.

The goal is to connect them, not necessarily collapse them.

### 9. What governance or privacy considerations should we keep in mind as we move toward a reporting solution?

**Data Governance Lead:**

Access should follow business need.

Leadership may need aggregate trends without customer-identifiable information.

Operational users may need record-level detail for complaints they are responsible for.

Compliance information may require additional restrictions.

We should identify which fields contain customer information or other sensitive information and determine whether those fields actually need to be included in the reporting layer.

We should also consider retention requirements and whether information from different systems has different retention rules.

Information Security and Compliance should ultimately validate the access model, but we can start by documenting what information is available and who says they need it.

### 10. What would success look like from a Data Governance perspective?

**Data Governance Lead:**

Success would mean that when someone says "complaint," we either know exactly which defined concept they mean or the reporting makes the distinction clear.

We'd have documented definitions, business rules, ownership, and relationships between the major concepts.

We'd know which systems are authoritative for which information.

Mappings between departmental and enterprise classifications would be documented and owned.

Changes to important definitions or classifications would follow a defined governance process rather than being made independently without understanding downstream impacts.

And importantly, the reporting would accurately represent the limitations of the data. If something is estimated, inferred, incomplete, or based on only certain channels, that should be clear.

### 11. Is there anything else you think I should know or investigate?

**Data Governance Lead:**

I would resist solving the governance problem entirely through technology.

Adding identifiers, improving integrations, and creating a shared data model can help, but they won't fix inconsistent human decisions by themselves.

Some of what you've uncovered points to process and training changes, particularly around when complaints should be captured, escalation criteria, and categorization.

I'd also recommend that you document the questions we still can't answer and validate them during data discovery.

For example, stakeholders believe there are duplicate records, missing complaints, and inconsistent categories. We should treat those as hypotheses until we've examined the actual data.

Finally, I wouldn't require every governance issue to be solved before delivering any value. We can establish a minimum set of definitions and controls needed for reliable initial reporting and create a roadmap for the remaining improvements.# Data Governance Lead Interview
